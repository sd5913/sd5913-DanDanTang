## 4. Important Design and Technical Decisions

- I started the 900 ms charge at physical wall contact. The enemy stays outside the wall and can be hit by towers during the charge. If the original wall disappears, the enemy can resume pursuit. A merge that preserves the wall's identity and contact keeps the existing timer.
- The original wall damage `B` is frozen when the spawn is queued. The full-health maximum is `Dmax = ceil(2.0 × B)`. An armored attacker that survives to impact deals `D = max(1, ceil(Dmax × currentHP / maxHP))`, using its health at resolution. Actual durability loss is capped by the wall's remaining HP.
- I added a visible charge cue, an estimated impact number that shows when it drops to a lower integer, and distinct tower-kill and wall-impact feedback. The wave summary counts nominal weakening on armored impacts that resolve. Actual damage is recorded separately.
- I kept the ordinary enemy, economy, and tower rules from the course baseline. The final defense still requires all 32 evacuation enemies to spawn and resolve. A charging enemy remains active until an eligible outcome.

## 5. Testing and Review Evidence

### Paid Challenge routes

The 26 November P0-K record covers scripted zero-resource runs using real excavation, technology purchases, crafting, and nearby placement. Seed 12 won with a paid four-wall, two-tower defense, resolving all 32 final enemies with 6 device HP remaining. A single-wall run on the same seed lost the device. Another armored enemy was killed by a tower before its charge ended. It dealt no wall damage and awarded one kill reward. The production browser checks also recorded the charge outside the wall, the changing estimate, and a retry during a live charge.

### Controlled comparisons and engineering checks

The 24 controlled runs matched eight prepared defenses across three rules. The layouts paired straight and corner approaches with four tower setups each. I compared the original impact, a stronger immediate impact, and the combined charge and health rule. The last comparison measures the charge and health rule together. The 900 ms candidate gave Piercing enough time to hold both tested lanes. The narrower approaches also queued attackers, increased opportunities for Burst splash, and reduced actual release rates by about 20–26% in these fixtures.

The prior P0-K acceptance record reports 356 passing unit tests, TypeScript checking, a production build, and automated Edge scenarios. The checks covered replacing the target wall, keeping the timer when a merge preserved the wall's identity, awarding a kill once, and waiting for the last active attacker before declaring evacuation complete. The standard production, targeted production, and controlled regression groups had distinct purposes and some overlapping coverage. This documentation revision reviewed those records.

A short fog-on comparison on the same machine and viewport measured about 13 seconds per build. The old build spawned 60 enemies and the candidate spawned 40. Frame intervals were similar in that sample. The active loads differed, so equal-load performance and full-length behavior remain open.

## 6. Problems Encountered

The charge changed the spacing and arrival rate in narrow lanes as well as giving towers a longer window to act. A slower queue can soften a wave or make clustered enemies easier targets for splash. I still need human observations to judge how that feels in normal play.

## 7. Changes to the Development Plan

I compared a 600 ms charge in eight matched fixtures after seeing the release-rate drop. It restored some throughput and preserved the corner Piercing win. The straight Piercing defense lost at 600 ms after winning at 900 ms. I kept 900 ms for the current private candidate because it retained an intervention window in both tested layouts. These fixture outcomes show a trade-off within the sampled defenses. The best timing for human play remains open.
